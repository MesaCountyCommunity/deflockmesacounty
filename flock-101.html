// The Basics

What Is Flock?

Flock Safety is a privately held operator of surveillance hardware and software, particularly automated license plate recognition (ALPR), mass video surveillance, gunfire locator systems, and supporting software to integrate the data gathered by these technologies. These cameras collect data on millions of vehicles regardless of whether the driver is suspected of a crime.

What is Flock Safety?
A private surveillance company that sells automated license plate readers (ALPRs) to police departments, HOAs, and private businesses. Each camera captures a photo of every passing vehicle: plate, make, color, distinguishing features, and timestamps the location.
What does Flock do in Mesa County?
Flock uses artificial intelligence to build a profile of your vehicle, taking into account make, model, color, bumper stickers, dents, and other distinguishing features, along with a timestamp. This data is logged into a massive nationwide database that can be searched by any Flock-connected agency. When an officer searches the Flock database with a plate, partial plate, or vehicle description, an image from every camera that has seen a matching vehicle is returned, allowing the authorities to build movement histories.

Officers can also perform “FreeForm” searches of the Flock database, where images of specific people are returned using searches such as “heavy-set male with a black and white hat” or “person on skateboard”. Recently, Flock introduced a Pan-Tilt-Zoom (PTZ) camera model that captures live video, tracks specific individuals, and can zoom in far enough to clearly read individual’s phone screens. PTZ cameras have been seen in Lincoln Park in Grand Junction.
Who can search the data?
Any agency with a Flock account, by default. That's thousands of police departments, sheriff's offices, federal agencies, and university PDs. Searches happen without warrants, without notification, and (here) without recorded reasons.
What does a "search" actually do?
An officer enters a plate, a partial plate, or a vehicle description. Flock returns every camera that's seen a matching vehicle, plotted on a map with timestamps. Repeat searches build movement histories: where someone goes, when, and how often.

// Why Should I Care

The Dangers of ALPRS

Privacy Issues
ALPRs track your movements and store your data for long periods of time, creating a detailed record of your location history. The question isn't whether you have something to hide, it's who gets to decide what counts as worth looking at, and when. A location history is a record of which doctor you see, which church or union hall you attend, whose house you stayed at overnight, and which protest you parked near. None of that is hidden.
Risk of Abuse
Data from ALPRs has led to wrongful arrests, profiling, watching children, and stalking ex-partners by police officers. List of known misuse cases
Your information that's collected into the database is just one bad actor away from being misused, and one data leak away from becoming public.
Limited Benefit
There's no substantial evidence that ALPRs effectively prevent crime, despite Flock's attempts to prove otherwise. Flock has known accuracy issues: in one California town, Flock misread license plates in 71% of the alerts it sent to police. Innocent people caught in the Flock surveillance dragnet have found it difficult to prove their innocence, and these encounters with police can turn violent.

While the Mesa County Sheriff's Office says the camera systems have contributed greatly to producing solid leads or solved cases, there is no evidence that the cameras have prevented crime or actually increased clearance rates. In fact, in Atlanta where they have an astounding 28,000 cameras, of which ~5,000 are Flock, there has been no discernable change in their clearance rates.
