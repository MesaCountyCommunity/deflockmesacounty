Flock 101

What automated license plate readers actually do, why a camera on a pole in Grand Junction is a national database question, and what any of it has to do with you.

What is an ALPR?

An automated license plate reader is a camera that photographs every passing vehicle, reads the plate with software, and writes a timestamped record — plate, location, direction, and usually a photo of the vehicle — into a searchable database. It does this to every car, continuously, with no suspicion of anything.

Flock Safety is the largest vendor selling these to local police departments and homeowners' associations in the United States. It is not the only one, and this campaign is about the technology and the contracts, not one company's name.

Why "just a camera" undersells it

A single camera records one road. The product being sold is the network: cameras from thousands of agencies feeding one system that any participating agency can search. That turns a local traffic record into a national location history.

Two things follow from that, and they are the whole argument:

  • Everyone is in it. The database is built from every vehicle that passes, not from suspects. Being in it requires nothing but driving.
  • The searching is done by strangers. Most searches of a local agency's cameras are typically run by other agencies, often in other states. The local department that installed the camera is not the one deciding who looks.

The common objections

"I have nothing to hide."

The question isn't whether you have something to hide, it's who gets to decide what counts as worth looking at, and when. A location history is a record of which doctor you see, which church or union hall you attend, whose house you stayed at overnight, and which protest you parked near. None of that is hidden. All of it is nobody's business by default.

"It solves crimes."

Sometimes it contributes to one. The honest version of this argument requires numbers — how many searches, how many arrests, how many convictions — and those numbers are what public records requests are for. "There are rules about who can access it."

There are policies. A policy is a document the agency writes for itself and can revise. What you can do about it

  • Find out what's deployed near you. The crowdsourced map at deflock.me shows reported ALPR locations, and you can add one you've spotted.
  • Ask for the records. Colorado's open records laws are how the audit logs on this site were obtained. The CORA toolkit has request language you can send today.
  • Tell the people who signed the contract. The contact tool builds an email to your council or commissioners in about two minutes.
  • Show up. Public comment is where contracts get questioned on the record. There are scripts if you'd rather not write one.
